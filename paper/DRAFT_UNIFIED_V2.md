# When Is One Flow Enough? Learning Damage and Restoration as Opposing Neural Flows

**Working AISTATS draft.** The theoretical and experimental center is the one-flow-versus-two-flow model-selection problem. Event shift is treated as an extension, not the headline. Unified-run result cells remain intentionally blank until the locked experiment is complete.

## Abstract

Many bounded states rise and fall because two nonnegative processes operate simultaneously, yet predictive models often collapse their net effect into one signed update. We ask when that simplification is harmless and when damage/interruption and restoration should be modeled as separate flows. For

\[
\mathbb E[Y_{t+1}-Y_t\mid X_t=x,Y_t=y]
=D(x)(1-y)-R(x)y,
\]

we derive the exact population loss of the best one-flow model. If

\[
A(x)=\mathbb E[(1-Y)^2\mid X=x],
\quad
B(x)=\mathbb E[Y^2\mid X=x],
\quad
v(x)=\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x),
\]

then collapsing to one signed, state-scaled flow incurs oracle error

\[
G(x)=v(x)
\min\left\{
\frac{R(x)^2}{A(x)},
\frac{D(x)^2}{B(x)}
\right\}.
\]

Thus one flow is sufficient exactly when only one physical flow is active or the conditional state is degenerate; otherwise two flows have a strict representation advantage. A fixed-design Gaussian result then gives an exact representation-estimation threshold: an oracle one-flow fit has expected fitted-mean error \(G_n+\sigma^2/n\), whereas a two-flow fit has error \(2\sigma^2/n\). Two flows are therefore favored when \(nG_n/\sigma^2>1\). Conditional state variance also controls whether the two flows can be learned separately, since the conditional Gram determinant equals \(v(x)\) and its smallest eigenvalue lies between \(v(x)\) and \(2v(x)\). Event shift appears as a secondary result: a one-flow projection learned under one event distribution receives an additional nonnegative projection-shift penalty on another. We instantiate the model with two small neural rate networks and evaluate it on eleven public county power-outage events under leave-one-event-out prediction. One main table compares the two-flow model with a parameter-matched one-flow network, gradient boosting on identical information, and damped persistence; one controlled phase-diagram ablation verifies when the additional flow is statistically worth estimating.

## 1. Introduction

An observed trajectory records only a net change. That net change may nevertheless be generated by two simultaneous processes. During a storm, some customers lose service while others are restored. In an epidemic, individuals enter and leave a compartment. In inventory systems, replenishment and demand act at the same time. A common predictive shortcut replaces the two nonnegative flows by one signed function.

That shortcut raises a model-selection question that is more basic than any particular architecture:

> When is one signed flow sufficient for prediction, and when does separating damage and restoration provide enough representation benefit to justify estimating an additional function?

This question has two distinct parts. First, are two flows **needed**? Even a perfect one-flow predictor may have irreducible approximation error when damage and restoration are both active. Second, are two flows **learnable and worth estimating**? The extra function increases variance, and a parsimonious one-flow model can predict better in small or noisy samples even when the data-generating mechanism has two flows.

We study the bounded-state transition

\[
Y_{t+1}
=Y_t+D(X_t)(1-Y_t)-R(X_t)Y_t+\varepsilon_t,
\tag{1}
\]

where \(D,R\ge0\). In the outage application, \(D\) is the interruption or damage flow and \(R\) is the restoration flow. The factors \(1-Y_t\) and \(Y_t\) are the populations exposed to each transition. The proposed model uses two neural functions. The structural comparator uses one signed neural function with the same state scaling and approximately the same number of parameters.

The paper makes three connected contributions.

1. **When one flow is sufficient and when two are necessary.** We derive the exact oracle approximation gap of the best one-flow model. It is zero if and only if damage is absent, restoration is absent, or the conditional state distribution is degenerate. When both flows are active and the state varies, one flow is structurally misspecified.
2. **When the second flow is statistically worth estimating.** In a local fixed-design Gaussian benchmark, two-flow and oracle one-flow fitted risks cross at \(nG_n/\sigma^2=1\). This gives a precise representation-estimation phase diagram. A supporting identification theorem shows that conditional state variance also controls whether the two functions can be separated from data.
3. **One theory-aligned empirical study.** We evaluate a deliberately simple two-flow neural model under complete event holdout. The main comparison is two flows versus a parameter-matched one-flow collapse. Gradient boosting and damped persistence provide external accuracy baselines. A single controlled synthetic ablation varies the quantities appearing in the theory and verifies the predicted crossover.

Event transfer is important but subordinate. A collapsed one-flow coefficient depends on the state distribution under which it is fitted, so changing events can add a projection-shift penalty. This strengthens the reason to retain two flows in some environments, but the paper does not claim that event holdout must always make the empirical two-flow gain larger.

## 2. Two-flow and one-flow prediction classes

Fix a driver value \(x\). The two-flow conditional drift is

\[
m_x(y)=D_0(x)(1-y)-R_0(x)y,
\qquad D_0(x),R_0(x)\ge0.
\tag{2}
\]

Define

\[
\mathcal F_2(x)
=\{d(1-y)-r y:d,r\ge0\}.
\]

The one-flow class retains the same state scaling but permits only one direction at a fixed \(x\):

\[
\mathcal F_1(x)
=\{a(1-y):a\ge0\}
\cup
\{-b y:b\ge0\}.
\tag{3}
\]

A member of \(\mathcal F_1\) may predict damage for some driver values and restoration for others. What it cannot represent is concurrent positive damage and restoration at the same driver value. This is the population counterpart of the `net_scaled` neural baseline.

For an environment \(e\), define

\[
A_e(x)=\mathbb E_e[(1-Y)^2\mid X=x],
\quad
B_e(x)=\mathbb E_e[Y^2\mid X=x],
\]

\[
C_e(x)=\mathbb E_e[Y(1-Y)\mid X=x],
\quad
v_e(x)=\operatorname{Var}_e(Y\mid X=x).
\]

Because \(Y\in[0,1]\),

\[
A_eB_e-C_e^2=v_e.
\tag{4}
\]

## 3. When is one flow enough?

### Theorem 1: exact one-flow oracle gap

At fixed \(x\),

\[
\boxed{
\inf_{g\in\mathcal F_1(x)}
\mathbb E_e[(m_x(Y)-g(Y))^2\mid X=x]
=
v_e(x)
\min\left\{
\frac{R_0(x)^2}{A_e(x)},
\frac{D_0(x)^2}{B_e(x)}
\right\}.
}
\tag{5}
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\text{one flow is population sufficient}
\iff
v_e(x)=0
\ \text{or}\ 
D_0(x)=0
\ \text{or}\ 
R_0(x)=0.
}
\tag{6}
\]

When \(v_e(x)>0\), \(D_0(x)>0\), and \(R_0(x)>0\), the gap is strictly positive.

#### Proof

For the damage-only branch,

\[
L_D(a)=\mathbb E_e[((D_0-a)(1-Y)-R_0Y)^2\mid X=x].
\]

Its unconstrained minimizer and minimum are

\[
a_e^*=D_0-R_0C_e/A_e,
\qquad
L_D(a_e^*)=R_0^2v_e/A_e.
\]

For the restoration-only branch,

\[
b_e^*=R_0-D_0C_e/B_e,
\qquad
L_R(b_e^*)=D_0^2v_e/B_e.
\]

At least one branch minimizer is nonnegative when \(v_e>0\). If, for example, \(a_e^*<0\), then \(D_0/R_0<C_e/A_e\le\sqrt{B_e/A_e}\), implying \(D_0^2/B_e<R_0^2/A_e\); the feasible restoration branch is therefore the minimizing branch. The symmetric argument covers \(b_e^*<0\). Taking the smaller feasible branch risk gives (5).

### Interpretation

The theorem separates three regimes.

- **Damage-only regime:** \(R_0(x)=0\). A damage flow is the correct model.
- **Restoration-only regime:** \(D_0(x)=0\). A restoration flow is the correct model.
- **Concurrent regime:** \(D_0(x),R_0(x)>0\). Two flows are representationally necessary whenever the same driver value is observed at more than one state.

The theorem does not yet say which fitted model predicts better. That requires accounting for estimation cost.

## 4. When is the second flow worth estimating?

Consider a local fixed-design regression with states \(y_1,\ldots,y_n\):

\[
Z_i=D_0(1-y_i)-R_0y_i+\varepsilon_i,
\qquad
\varepsilon_i\stackrel{iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).
\tag{7}
\]

Let \(m\in\mathbb R^n\) be the noiseless conditional mean vector. Let \(P_2\) project onto the two-flow design span, and let \(P_1^*\) be the better of the damage-only and restoration-only rank-one projections. Define

\[
G_n=\frac1n\|(I-P_1^*)m\|_2^2.
\tag{8}
\]

### Theorem 2: fixed-design flow-selection threshold

If the two-flow design has rank two,

\[
\mathbb E\left[
\frac1n\|P_2Z-m\|_2^2
\middle|y_1,\ldots,y_n
\right]
=
\frac{2\sigma^2}{n},
\tag{9}
\]

whereas the oracle one-flow fit satisfies

\[
\mathbb E\left[
\frac1n\|P_1^*Z-m\|_2^2
\middle|y_1,\ldots,y_n
\right]
=
G_n+rac{\sigma^2}{n}.
\tag{10}
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\text{two flow predicts the fitted mean better}
\iff
\Gamma_n:=\frac{nG_n}{\sigma^2}>1.
}
\tag{11}
\]

This result uses an oracle branch for the one-flow model and therefore does not disadvantage it.

#### Proof

Since \(m\) lies in the rank-two design span,

\[
P_2Z-m=P_2\varepsilon,
\]

and the expected squared norm is \(\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(P_2)=2\sigma^2\). For one flow,

\[
P_1^*Z-m=-(I-P_1^*)m+P_1^*\varepsilon.
\]

The two terms are orthogonal, so the expected squared norm is \(nG_n+\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(P_1^*)=nG_n+\sigma^2\). Dividing by \(n\) yields (9)–(11).

### Neural interpretation

For neural conditional functions, the extra estimation cost is governed by effective complexity rather than exactly one scalar parameter. The robust principle is

\[
\boxed{
\text{choose two flows when their reduction in approximation error exceeds the additional estimation error.}
}
\tag{12}
\]

The exact local result supplies a benchmark and a phase diagram; it is not presented as an exact formula for neural-network generalization risk.

## 5. Can the two flows be identified?

Define

\[
\phi(Y)=\begin{bmatrix}1-Y\\-Y\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
Q_e(x)=\mathbb E_e[\phi(Y)\phi(Y)^\top\mid X=x].
\]

### Theorem 3: identification geometry

\[
\boxed{
\det Q_e(x)=v_e(x),
\qquad
v_e(x)\le\lambda_{\min}(Q_e(x))\le2v_e(x).
}
\tag{13}
\]

Thus \(D_0(x)\) and \(R_0(x)\) are locally identified if and only if \(v_e(x)>0\).

This theorem answers a different question from Theorem 1:

- Theorem 1 measures whether one flow loses predictive expressiveness.
- Theorem 3 measures whether two flows can be statistically separated.
- Theorem 2 balances representation benefit against finite-sample cost.

Together they yield the flow-selection phase diagram:

| Regime | Oracle gap | Identification | Preferred predictive structure |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| \(D=0\) or \(R=0\) | zero | remaining flow estimable | one flow |
| \(v=0\) | zero | two flows not separable | one flow |
| \(G>0\), \(\Gamma_n<1\) | positive but weak | possibly weak/noisy | one flow can win by parsimony |
| \(G>0\), \(\Gamma_n>1\) | positive and sufficiently large | sufficient information | two flows favored |

## 6. Event shift as an extension

A one-flow coefficient is a projection and therefore depends on the state distribution. For the damage-only branch fitted under source event \(P\),

\[
a_P^*=D_0-R_0\frac{C_P}{A_P}.
\]

On target event \(Q\),

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_Q^+(a_P^*)
=
R_0^2\frac{v_Q}{A_Q}
+
R_0^2A_Q
\left(
\frac{C_P}{A_P}-\frac{C_Q}{A_Q}
\right)^2.
}
\tag{14}
\]

The first term is the target oracle one-flow gap in Theorem 1; the second is an additional event-projection-shift penalty. The restoration branch is symmetric.

Equation (14) explains why distribution shift can make one flow more fragile. It is not the paper's central theorem, and it does not imply that a finite-sample two-flow neural model must win on every held-out event.

## 7. Neural model

The proposed predictor keeps the architecture deliberately simple:

\[
D_\theta(x)=c_D\sigma(f_D(x;\theta_D)),
\qquad
R_\theta(x)=c_R\sigma(f_R(x;\theta_R)),
\]

\[
\widehat Y_{t+1}
=
\widehat Y_t+D_\theta(X_t)(1-\widehat Y_t)-R_\theta(X_t)\widehat Y_t.
\tag{15}
\]

Both networks are two-layer ReLU MLPs of width 32. The `net_scaled` comparator is a parameter-matched signed network:

\[
\widehat Y_{t+1}
=
\widehat Y_t+n_\theta(X_t)_+(1-\widehat Y_t)
-n_\theta(X_t)_-\widehat Y_t.
\tag{16}
\]

Equation (16) can switch direction across \(x\), but it cannot keep both flows positive at the same \(x\). No semiparametric component, event embedding, recurrent memory, damage gate, or second damage head is included.

## 8. One empirical study

### Data and protocol

The primary cohort contains all eleven events in `g2-convective-11`. Each outer fold holds out one complete event. The event is the inferential unit, and neural seeds are averaged within event. The primary forecast window is 24 hours, with h+6 and h+24 endpoints.

### Main comparison

The single main table contains:

1. two-flow neural dynamics;
2. parameter-matched one-flow `net_scaled`;
3. HGB using the same available information;
4. damped persistence.

The first contrast tests the theory. HGB and persistence establish practical forecasting context.

### Training objective

The model is trained with equal event weight using

\[
L
=
\frac1{|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{train}}|}
\sum_e
\left[
\frac12L_{\mathrm{roll},e}^{1:24}
+
\frac12L_{\mathrm{step},e}^{1:24}
\right].
\tag{17}
\]

The teacher-forced term directly estimates the conditional drift in the theory; it is an optimization choice rather than an independent contribution.

## 10. Discussion

The theory does not promote two flows unconditionally. It states that two flows earn their additional complexity only in a concurrent, state-dispersed, sufficiently informative regime. A one-flow predictor is the correct choice when one physical process is absent, when the data cannot distinguish the processes, or when the representation gain is smaller than the estimation penalty.

Power-outage data occupy both sides of this boundary. Damage and restoration may coexist, but restoration is weakly informed near zero outage and independent events are scarce. This makes the application scientifically useful: it exposes both the structural benefit and the statistical cost of decomposition.

Event shift can enlarge the one-flow approximation loss because the signed projection changes with the conditional state distribution. The corrected empirical evidence nevertheless shows heterogeneity across events and horizons, so this extension is presented as a risk decomposition rather than a dominance claim.

The current paper deliberately stops before adding memory, semi-structured terms, or richer sequence architectures. Those modifications may improve absolute accuracy, but they would obscure the more fundamental comparison between one net flow and two opposing flows.

## 11. Claims and limitations

**Claimed:**

- an exact criterion for when one flow is population sufficient;
- an exact local finite-sample threshold for when the second flow repays its variance cost;
- an identification result for separating the two flow functions;
- one event-held-out neural comparison of two flows, one flow, and external baselines.

**Not claimed:**

- that two flows always outperform one flow;
- that event holdout necessarily increases the two-flow advantage;
- that identified neural rates are causal damage or crew-productivity parameters;
- that conditional state variance explains event-family performance ordering;
- that the simple two-flow model dominates HGB at long horizons;
- that a second outage rise requires a second damage network.
